This is one independent LLM participant's live authoring study, not evidence of preferences shared by LLMs generally. I used only discovery and the exposed editor MCP tools for scene work through `/tmp/katla-material-surface-call.py`. I did not inspect implementation code, shader sources, or other study reports, and did not change source, git, preferences, or processes. The parent identified the binary as a517de0f plus the new surface-factor change; shader sources were held fixed. All scene edits were in the authorized disposable editor.

The session completed 45 recorded participant calls: one tool discovery and 44 exposed-tool calls. Three calls intentionally failed. The complete ordered record is `transcript.jsonl`; numbered references below mean its 1-based lines. Fifteen returned native viewport images are preserved as `view-0.png` through `view-14.png`, and all were visually inspected. The saved scene is `authored-materials.katla`. `image-comparisons.json` contains pixel comparisons computed from the original PNG RGB samples without modifying the images. The parent's health check is not counted.

Discovery was sufficient to author ordinary and self-emissive materials without source knowledge. The `material` schema says to request presets first, names its supported actions, identifies sRGB base RGB and linear alpha, and defines base color, metallic, roughness, AO, and occlusion strength in 0..1. Emission is linear RGB, nonnegative, explicitly allows HDR values above 1, and works without an emissive texture. Normal scale is described as the tangent-space X/Y multiplier, with zero flattening the normal map, but no legal range or explanation of negative scales appears in the schema. The presets receipt repeats the color spaces, maximum batch size 256, atomic batching, per-object multiplier scope, preservation of geometry and textures, and `textures_editable: false`. It explicitly says presets provide isotropic factors rather than texture installation or directional brushing. Alpha is explicitly not a render-mode switch. These boundaries prevented me from attempting unsupported texture replacement or transparency.

`query_entities` returned named objects, bounds, decimal IDs, and an explicit `material_editable` flag. It identified the scene's 27 mesh objects as editable and its directional light as uneditable. This was enough to distinguish sphere objects from their plinths by name, position, and bounds. The material is not listed among each row's registered components, so the flag and dedicated material tool are essential. `editor_view` supplied a fresh native image, camera, projected candidates, and center GPU pick, while carefully disclaiming occlusion visibility. I used the image and names to choose the targets. Discovery also correctly directed me to `search_assets` rather than invented filenames, then to `spawn_model` and its returned `material_entity_ids`.

The native appearance matched my intended contrast. The cyan sphere's lower hemisphere remains strongly colored and bright rather than showing the dark underside of its non-emissive neighbor. The terracotta sphere retains directional shading and a subdued highlight. Views 0 and 1 show the original materials and authored result at the same camera. A temporary emission-only patch `[3,0,0]` gave a red/magenta sphere in view 2; undo restored cyan in view 3, keeping the other factors and neighboring sphere intact. No bloom halo or illumination cast onto nearby objects was verified or promised. Emission here is demonstrated as a visible surface contribution, not established as an area-light source.

For error and recovery, #10 submitted a batch targeting valid sphere 11 plus the known non-mesh light 28. It returned `Entity Entity(28:0) has no rendered material; choose a mesh object`. Inspect #11 proved the valid sphere still had its original cyan emission, demonstrating rejection without a partial edit in this case. Retrying the same red emission on sphere 11 alone succeeded (#12). Undo (#14) restored the previous numeric material (#15) and appearance (view 3). This was a deliberate diagnostic error rather than an accidental ambiguous-target edit.

A second diagnostic used roughness 1.4 (#25). The tool rejected it with `Material color and factors must be finite numbers in 0..=1`. The next valid multi-factor patch succeeded (#26) with a before receipt showing roughness was still 1.0. The rejection is useful, but its wording is too broad: the same interface explicitly permits HDR emissive factors, and I successfully authored emission 3 and 2. An agent seeing only the error could incorrectly clamp emission. I prefer a structured field-specific validation error, such as field `roughness`, provided 1.4, supported range [0,1], with `batch_applied: false` where relevant.

The textured imported model was discoverable and usable. `search_assets` listed `models/DamagedHelmet.glb` and documented its resource-relative path contract. `spawn_model` returned root 29 and material target `[29]` (#16), avoiding root-versus-child guesswork. The focused native frame (view 4) clearly showed detailed worn metal, patterned visor, and turquoise emissive markings. Material inspection returned its imported factors (white base, metallic 1, roughness 1, emission [1,1,1], normal and occlusion scales 1). It did not report whether any texture slot was bound, nor texture IDs, resource paths, UV sets, sampler state, or import provenance. Thus actual texture-slot presence was inferred from the detailed rendered model and asset identity, not independently confirmed by an inspection receipt.

The normal and occlusion controls affected native output separately. After setting both to zero (#19, view 5), restoring only normal scale to one (#21, view 6) changed 12,266 RGB pixels, all inside the helmet bounding rectangle [221,336,494,666]. Restoring only occlusion strength to one (#23, view 7) changed 19,576 RGB pixels inside [221,341,494,666]. Camera values remained constant; no scene operation intervened other than the factor patch and observe. These localized differences support both controls reaching rendered output on this model, although their visual impact was subtle at this framing. The final warm tint and emission retained patterned detail and changed the helmet's surface and luminous markings to amber (view 8). I did not test texture reassignment, UV editing, samplers, or anisotropic brushing because discovery exposes no such operations.

Save #29 and load #30 succeeded at the scratch scene path. Fresh name queries (#31–32) found sphere IDs 40 and 42 and helmet ID 58. Inspecting the old ID 11 (#33) correctly failed with `Entity 11 does not exist`. The fresh-ID inspections confirmed all authored factors. Views 9 and 10, captured before and after save/load at exactly the same camera, have zero differing RGB pixels out of 685,685. This verifies native persistence for the visible composed scene as well as the numeric factors; view 11 additionally focuses the reloaded helmet.

A final attempted isolation test discovered the reloaded daylight ID 57 and its intensity field (#39–40), changed intensity from 3 to 0 (#42), observed a frame (#43), and undid that change (#44). The restore inspection (#45) reports intensity 3. Views 12, 13, and 14 are pixel-identical. Therefore the light-field mutation's effect on rendered lighting was not established, and this attempt cannot serve as proof that the cyan material stays lit when scene lighting is disabled. I preserved the observation rather than attributing it to a renderer bug or changing shader sources. All saved authored materials remain intact, and the editor light was restored.

My preferred interface improvements, based on this session rather than claims about LLMs generally, are: expose actual texture slots and provenance in material inspection; include a compact example showing the different base-color and emission color spaces; state normal-scale legal and practical ranges and finite-value limits; return field-specific validation errors; add an optional editable-only query filter; and link material set receipts to the committed frame/submission that incorporated the change. Texture inspection should distinguish missing slots, built-in fallback samples, imported image resources, and object-factor overrides, so I can explain why a control has or lacks a visible effect without reading shaders. Texture authoring, when supported, should use discovered resource handles and identify the selected primitive/slot explicitly. A reusable emissive preset or color-plus-intensity convenience input would help my authoring workflow, while retaining the existing exact linear-RGB factor form.

The existing explicit discovery, partial patches, before/after receipts, material-target IDs, atomic rejection, undo, native viewport capture, and fresh-ID rediscovery already formed a workable authoring loop. I could verify colored surface emission, ordinary PBR shading, imported detail preservation, separate normal/occlusion rendering changes, numeric persistence, and pixel-identical scene reload. I could not verify Metal behavior, absence of textures on the primitive sphere, emission as a light source, or a rendered response to the directional-light component mutation. This study contains one participant and one disposable live session; it does not measure broader LLM reliability or usability statistically.
